Quoted but Not Booked? Treatment Plan Follow-Up for UK Dental Practices

7 MIN READ·OriFlows Resources

A patient listens carefully, nods, and says they will think about it. The treatment plan is quoted, the conversation feels positive, and then nothing happens. No booking, no call back, and no clear reason why.

That silence is easy to read as a rejection. In a UK private dental practice, it usually is not. It is far more often a stalled decision: cost to consider, time to arrange, a partner to talk to, nerves to settle, or simply no next step booked before the patient left the practice.

This article sets out a polite, timed follow-up sequence for quoted treatment plans. It is not a hard sell, and it does not replace your treatment coordinator. It gives a patient who was interested a clear, easy way back to booking.

Why treatment plans stall

Most plans do not stall because the patient disliked the treatment. They stall because the next step was never made concrete. Common reasons include:

  • Cost and timing. The patient needs to check finances, or to fit treatment around work, holidays, or family commitments.
  • A second opinion in their own head. They want time to talk it over at home, especially for higher-value treatment.
  • Nerves. A patient can agree in principle and still need reassurance before committing to a procedure.
  • No booked next step. “Let us know” puts all of the work back on the patient, and busy patients rarely come back on their own.

None of these need pressure. They need a short, helpful reminder, a chance to ask a question, and a booking link that makes the next step take seconds rather than another phone call.

A follow-up sequence that respects the patient

The sequence below is a guide, not a rigid script. Adjust the timing to your practice, and always let a human take over when a patient asks a clinical question or raises a concern.

Step 1: the same-day summary

After the consultation, send a short summary in the practice’s voice: what was discussed, the next step, and how to book it. This is not a sales message. It is a record the patient can re-read at home, without relying on memory.

Step 2: a check-in a few days later

A few days later, one polite check-in works better than silence: “Did you have any questions about the plan we discussed?” If the patient replies, a member of the team answers. Automated messages should open conversations, not answer clinical questions.

Step 3: a clear next step, about two weeks later

If there is still no booking, offer one concrete next step, such as a short review appointment or a booking link for the treatment itself. One clear option beats a long list of choices.

Step 4: close the loop, and leave the door open

After two or three touches, stop chasing. A final, courteous message can say the plan remains available and the patient is welcome to book when ready. That respects the patient, protects your reputation, and keeps the relationship intact for a future visit or recall.

What to include, and what to leave out

Include only what helps the patient decide: the treatment discussed, the next appointment or booking link, and payment or finance information only if your practice actually offers it. Do not invent offers, discounts, or deadlines that do not exist.

Leave clinical advice to clinicians. If a patient asks whether a treatment is right for them, whether pain is normal, or whether they should proceed, that conversation belongs with your team, not with an automated message.

When to stop following up

Stop immediately if a patient asks not to be contacted, says no, or opts out. Also pause follow-up if a patient says “not now” and gives a reason, such as a holiday or a medical matter. Note the reason, agree a sensible time to revisit if appropriate, and do not keep messaging in the meantime.

Stopping well is part of good follow-up. A practice that respects a “no” is far more likely to be chosen again than one that keeps chasing.

How the team stays in control

Good follow-up is a list with an owner, not a set of messages firing into the void. For each quoted plan, your team should be able to see:

  • What was quoted, and when
  • What has already been sent, and what the patient replied
  • Who is responsible for the next contact
  • Whether the plan is booked, declined, paused, or still open

That visibility is what turns follow-up from “someone should chase that” into a system. It also prevents the most common embarrassment: two people from the same practice contacting the same patient about the same plan.

Measure your own quoted-to-booked rate

Before changing anything, count your own figures for a month: how many treatment plans were quoted, how many were booked, and how many went quiet with no reply. That quoted-to-booked rate is your baseline. It will vary by treatment type, price, and practice, so do not judge it against a generic industry percentage.

After introducing a follow-up sequence, compare the same measure. If more quoted plans are booked, and patients are not complaining about being chased, the system is working. If bookings do not move, the problem may be earlier in the journey, such as how the plan or the next step is explained at the consultation itself.

Frequently asked questions

Is treatment plan follow-up pushy? Not when it is short, timed, and easy to stop. One summary, one check-in, and one clear next step is helpful. Repeated daily messages are not.

Does this work for high-value treatment? Often it matters more there, because high-value decisions take longer and involve other people. The follow-up should give the patient time and information, not urgency they did not ask for.

Does follow-up replace the treatment coordinator? No. It supports them by making sure no quoted plan is simply forgotten. Clinical questions, consent, and final decisions always stay with your team.

What if a patient never replies? Close the loop politely after a few touches, keep the plan on record, and let a future recall or check-up bring the conversation back naturally.
